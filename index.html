THE DECISION FRAME

The analysis had to answer more than “who is risky?”

A lender needs evidence for where to focus limited underwriting attention without treating a statistical label as an automatic decision.

01
Where is the risk concentrated?

Prioritize material segments, not just the highest percentages in tiny groups.

02
What is the model getting wrong?

Missed high-risk profiles and unnecessary reviews create different operational consequences.

03
How would decisions change?

A review threshold controls queue volume, but not whether a new rule is safe or fair.

01 / RESEARCH & FINDINGS

The patterns that changed the recommendation.

Three findings helped move the project from exploratory analysis toward a focused review strategy.

FINDING 01

Same volume. Very different risk concentration.

New Car: 65 high-risk classifications among 104 borrowers (62.5%). Small Appliance: 42 among 105 (40.0%). The counts, not merely the rates, show why this difference deserves attention.

Decision implication: Investigate the New Car segment first instead of imposing blanket restrictions across all purposes.

FINDING 02

Longer relationships didn't guarantee lower risk.

Historical high-risk labels increased from 22.7% for customers of 0–12 months to 74.2% for customers of 49+ months. The association between relationship duration and risk labels was statistically significant (χ² = 29.778, p < 0.001).

Decision implication: Tenure alone is a poor shortcut for creditworthiness. Investigate which other variables may explain this relationship.

FINDING 03

One financial indicator couldn't tell the whole story.

Borrowers with positive checking and zero savings had a 26.5% high-risk rate (n = 49), while borrowers with both balances positive had a 56.0% rate (n = 125). These counterintuitive patterns challenge simple balance-based rules.

Decision implication: Assess combined financial profiles and repayment capacity, not a single account-balance threshold.

Choosing a model worth validating

Five-fold cross-validation accuracy, not a single lucky test split

Logistic Regression62.94%
Controlled Random Forest60.88%
Original Random Forest59.12%
Why Logistic Regression? The Random Forest variants did better on one holdout split, but Logistic Regression had the strongest average cross-validation accuracy and high-risk recall among the models tested. The original forest also showed pronounced overfitting.

These are exploratory model results—not deployment-ready underwriting performance.

04 / MANAGEMENT RECOMMENDATION

Test the decision process.
Don't change lending policy yet.

The available data support a supervised 90-day shadow pilot, not an automated credit-approval rule.

DAYS 01–30

Validate

Review inputs, risk labels, model errors, stability, and fairness before any operational use.

OUTPUT → Readiness criteria
DAYS 31–60

Shadow-test

Compare proposed review routes against existing processes, recording review volume and staff time while decisions remain unchanged.

OUTPUT → Operational evidence
DAYS 61–90

Evaluate

Assess missed risk, unnecessary reviews, burden, fairness, and any available repayment outcomes.

OUTPUT → Continue / revise / stop
Management decision requested: authorize validation and shadow-testing.

No automatic approvals, denials, or production threshold changes during the pilot.

What this analysis does—and doesn't—establish

The historical target is a credit-risk classification, not an observed default. These files don't include verified repayment performance, loan profitability, or the effects of alternative terms. Results are associative and exploratory. Production use would require external validation, compliance review, fairness testing, and human oversight.
